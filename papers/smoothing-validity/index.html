Technical note

Local Residual Smoothing and Exact Conformal Validity

A small-sample separation. Estimating a smoothly varying residual law well means borrowing from neighbouring inputs, and borrowing is exactly what an exact distribution-free guarantee forbids. The two are separated by a quantity of order \(h^s\) in the bandwidth, and the estimation-optimal bandwidth makes that quantity largest precisely when data are scarce.

Read the PDF

Abstract

Fix any model, and consider the remaining task: estimate the conditional law of its residual at a test point from held-out residuals. When that law varies smoothly with the input, the efficient estimator at a small sample size borrows from neighbours by kernel smoothing, with a bandwidth that widens as data thin. We show that this borrowing is incompatible with an exact, distribution-free, finite-sample coverage guarantee. Any test-centred smoothing incurs a coverage gap of the same order as its bias, so exact coverage forces the bandwidth to its widest, uniform pooling, which is the crudest estimate of the residual law. The two goals, efficient small-sample estimation and an exact guarantee, are separated by a quantity of order \(h^s\) in the bandwidth \(h\), and the estimation-optimal bandwidth makes that quantity largest exactly when data are scarce. The separation vanishes only as the sample grows or when the residual law does not vary with the input. Nothing here concerns the base model, which may be arbitrarily adaptive; the tension is entirely in the pooling step that supplies the guarantee.

The setup

Grant the strongest version of the standard defence of conformal prediction. The base model may be anything, fit with full access to labels on a training fold, and it is frozen before the analysis starts. It induces a residual \(R = A(X,Y)\), and the only question left is how to turn a held-out sample of residuals into a predictive statement at a fresh input. Write \(\mu_x = \mathcal{L}(R \mid X = x)\) for the conditional residual law. Two assumptions carry the argument, and for the interesting case both hold.

The first is smoothness: the residual law is Hölder-\(s\) in total variation, so \(d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu_x, \mu_{x'}) \le C\,\rho(x,x')^s\). This is what makes borrowing from neighbours pay, since nearby inputs have nearby residual laws. The second is heterogeneity: the residual law actually depends on the input. That is what makes the problem nontrivial, because without it a single pooled law is correct and there is nothing to estimate locally.

The two sides

Efficiency wants a narrow bandwidth, and wants it wider as the effective sample count falls. That is the ordinary bias-variance trade for a weighted empirical law, and it gives the minimax rate \(m^{-s/(2s+d)}\).

Validity wants the opposite. Asking the weighted estimator to carry a guarantee invokes the coverage bound for non-exchangeable weighted conformal prediction of Barber, Candès, Ramdas and Tibshirani: replacing the exchangeable reference by a covariate-weighted one costs, in coverage, the weighted total-variation distance between the calibration and test residual laws. Under smoothness with kernel weights of bandwidth \(h\) that cost is \(\Theta(h^s)\). The kernel weights are informative precisely because they are non-uniform in a way that depends on the test point, and that is exactly what the bound charges for.

Driving the gap to zero, absent a known covariate-shift tilt, means removing the test-centred dependence of the weights altogether. The endpoint is uniform pooling within a region pre-committed before the test point is seen, which is the widest possible reach and the largest possible bias.

The separation

Putting the two together, no predictor in this family attains both exact distribution-free coverage and the minimax estimation rate, unless the sample is unbounded or the residual law is input-free. The trade is at one order: a predictor within a constant of the minimax rate has a coverage gap of \(\Theta(m^{-s/(2s+d)})\), and a predictor with zero coverage gap pays the corresponding estimation risk. The efficient thing and the guaranteed thing are the two ends of one bandwidth, and at a small effective sample size they are far apart.

What it says in practice

With effective sample sizes well below a thousand, a kernel or local-likelihood estimate of the residual law is usually the better forecast, and it does not come with, and is not improved by, an exact conformal wrapper. The wrapper would either localize, and forfeit the exactness that is its entire justification, or pool, and forfeit the estimate. The separation closes only as data accumulate, when a narrow bandwidth is both efficient and nearly exchangeable, or when the residual law does not vary with the input, when there was never a reason to localize. Between those limits, in the small-sample heterogeneous regime that describes most applied forecasting, the guarantee and the estimate pull in opposite directions.

This is the same pooling cost that Marginally Useful prices as \(I(R;X)\), reached from the estimation side rather than the information side. There the question is what pooling costs you in log score against an oracle. Here it is what localizing costs you in coverage, and the answer is that the two costs are the same object seen from opposite ends.
