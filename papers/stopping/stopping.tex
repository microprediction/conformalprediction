\begin{abstract}
We view conformal prediction as a repairable univariate transform to be included alongside other time-series operators
in a forecasting grammar. By this device we evaluate the wisdom of stopping after the conformalizing step, something that
is tacit in conformal prediction and departed from by only two of the 623 papers proposing conformal methods in our survey. Across 17,364 economic time-series,
 early stopping costs approximately 0.4 nats. We interpret this through the lens of a new information identity we term the conformal information gap,
 whereby it is possible to formalize why conformal calibration cannot exploit forecastability. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The most any forecast of an outcome $Y$ can gain over the unconditional distribution
of $Y$, in expected log score, is the mutual information $\I(Y;X)$ between the outcome
and the information $X$ available to the forecaster \citep{delsole2004}.
\citet{catt2026} takes this quantity as the definition of the forecastability of $Y$,
and calls the gain a method actually achieves its exploitability. We ask how much
forecastability a conformal forecaster exploits, and we will prove that conformal
calibration exploits none of it. 

The conformal recipe is familiar. We fit a base model and then, on $n$ held-out points, compute a scalar error
for each (for instance the absolute residual). We then sort the errors, take the
$\lceil (n+1)\,0.9\rceil$-th smallest, and quote the model's prediction plus or
minus that value. If the joint law of the held-out data and the future point is
unchanged by permuting them, the quoted interval contains
the truth at least 90\% of the time, at any sample size, with no further assumption on
the model or the data distribution.

The last step of the recipe applies the empirical distribution function of the
held-out errors, and the quoted interval is read off its quantiles. Nothing is fitted
to the output of that step. The recipe stops there, and that is the reason we 
choose to describe conformal prediction as a form of early stopping. The stop
 is easy to miss because conformal prediction is usually presented as a
self-contained procedure. 

Once the empirical map is repaired, however, it is an
invertible univariate transform of the error (Section~\ref{sec:transform}). It then
belongs in a grammar of similar transforms, alongside subtracting a running mean,
dividing by a running scale, differencing, serial whitening, and the normal quantile
transform of hydrological forecasting \citep{bogner2012}. A forecaster working in that
grammar can apply the transform and keep modelling its output. As far as we know the
literature does not describe conformal prediction as early stopping, and no paper
justifies stopping at the empirical map. We show that the stop carries a steep
empirical price.

We make two contributions. We introduce and prove the conformal information gap
identity (Proposition~\ref{prop:floor}), which fixes, exactly and at a fixed
calibration sample, the part of the excess log score that no calibration choice can
reach. We then measure what stopping costs on economic series, and how much of that
cost composing a model past the pooled law recovers (Section~\ref{sec:price}). A
detailed census of 680 papers supports our characterization of the conformal
literature (Section~\ref{sec:count}).

\section{Conformal prediction as a univariate transform}\label{sec:transform}

Viewed as a univariate transform, conformal prediction is in need of repair. Its raw form is a step function, and once repaired it is an invertible map
that can sit anywhere in a chain of transforms.

Let $R_1,\dots,R_n$ be calibration scores, for example held-out residuals. Split
conformal prediction applies the fence-post empirical map
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fencepost}
E_n(r) \;=\; \frac{1+\#\{i\le n:\ R_i\le r\}}{n+1}
\end{equation}
and reads the prediction set off its level sets
\citep{vovk2005algorithmic,lei2018distribution}. The value of $E_n$ at a new score is
a conformal $p$-value, and $E_n$ itself is the unrandomized conformal predictive
distribution of \citet{vovk2019nonparametric}. If $R_1,\dots,R_{n+1}$ are
exchangeable and almost surely distinct, the rank of $R_{n+1}$ among all $n+1$
scores is uniform, so $E_n(R_{n+1})$ is uniform on the grid
$\{k/(n+1):k=1,\dots,n+1\}$. That is the coverage guarantee.

A \emph{transform}, in the sense of \citet{cotton2026transforms}, is an online map
with a state. Its forward function consumes an observation and the current state
and emits a transformed observation. Its inverse maps a predictive distribution for
the transformed observation back to one for the original. Given the state, the
forward function is a strictly increasing change of variables, so predictive
densities pull back exactly, Jacobians included. A forecaster is a \emph{chain} of
transforms followed by a \emph{leaf}, a predictive law for whatever the last
transform emits. Table~\ref{tab:transforms} lists the transforms of the
\texttt{skaters} library in which that grammar is implemented. Every one of them
is invertible given its state, and a chain may stack any of them in any order.

The map \eqref{eq:fencepost} is a step function. It has no inverse and no density,
so it is not a transform. \citet{cotton2026grammar} repairs it by interpolation. Let
$r_{(1)}<\dots<r_{(n)}$ be the order statistics of the scores, give the $i$-th knot
the fence-post probability $i/(n+1)$, and set $z_{(i)}=\Phi^{-1}\big(i/(n+1)\big)$.
The repaired map $m_n$ is any strictly increasing bijection of the real line with
$m_n(r_{(i)})=z_{(i)}$ for $i=1,\dots,n$, rebuilt as new scores arrive.
Appendix~\ref{app:repair} gives the interpolation and tails used here. The repair
keeps the certificate at the knots, $\Pr\{m_n(R_{n+1})\le z_{(i)}\}=i/(n+1)$, and
$\Phi(m_n(R_{n+1}))$ is within Kolmogorov distance $1/(n+1)$ of uniform whatever the
interpolation (Appendix~\ref{app:repair}).

With $m_n$ in hand the two positions are precise. A forecaster \emph{stops} if $m_n$
is the last transform in its chain and the leaf is the fixed standard normal law.
Its predictive is then $N(0,1)$ pulled back through $m_n$, which is the smoothed
empirical law of the calibration scores: the conformal predictive, and nothing
downstream of it. A forecaster \emph{composes} if it keeps $m_n$ and fits further
transforms or a leaf to the stream $z_t=m_n(R_t)$, then pulls the result back
through $m_n$. Composing keeps the certificate at the position of $m_n$. It does not
carry the certificate to the final predictive, which is the price of continuing.

\section{How often published methods stop at the pooled law}\label{sec:count}

We ask what published conformal methods do with the fence-post map
\eqref{eq:fencepost}, which we also call the \emph{empirical map}. A method
\emph{stops} if the output of the map, its quantiles, its $p$-values or its
intervals, is the answer. It \emph{composes} if it keeps the map and fits a model to
its output, as in Section~\ref{sec:transform}.

Methods that neither stop nor compose fall into three further classes. A method
\emph{replaces} if it substitutes a conditional estimate for the pooled quantile, as
SPCI fits a quantile regression to recent residuals \citep{xu2023spci}. It
\emph{tunes} if it keeps the map and adapts the level online, as adaptive conformal
inference does \citep{gibbs2021adaptive}. It \emph{consumes} if it feeds the output
to another procedure, such as training on pseudo-labels it has screened or selecting
among candidates.

We classified the literature instead of relying on an impression of it. We took every arXiv paper with ``conformal prediction'' in the title as of
20 August 2026, 686 records. Six concern molecular or protein conformation and were
matched by the title search in error, which leaves the remaining 680 papers. Language-model
readers (Claude Sonnet) coded each paper against a written rubric, read its body in
full, and recorded the sentence that places it.\footnote{Two readers, each blind to the other, read the body of
every paper from its first line to its reference list. We read the method sections of
every paper on which they disagree, and of every paper either of them coded as
composing, and settled its label. An earlier pass that read only the abstract, the
introduction and the method passages found the same two composing papers. Four papers
have no PDF on arXiv and were coded from their abstracts. The rubric and the per-paper
labels of both readers, with their quoted evidence, are in the repository.}

Of the 680 papers, 454 stop, 77 consume, 50 replace, 40 tune, and 57 propose no
method. The two that compose do so narrowly. \citet{messoudi2021copula} and
\citet{park2024semiparametric} fit a copula to the outputs of per-target empirical
maps, but use it only to set each target's quantile level and still return a box of
conformal intervals. No paper models the rank stream of a single outcome or carries a
fitted law back as a forecast. \citet{lardy2024anytime} come closest, fitting
densities to conformal ranks, but to drive a test martingale.

Distributional conformal prediction \citep{chernozhukov2021distributional} is the
nearest single-outcome case. It conditions \emph{before} the map, and its answer is
the empirical quantile of the resulting ranks. A paper would count as composing if it
kept the empirical map, applied it to scores, and fitted a model on the transformed
stream. Reweighting the empirical law, conditioning upstream and adapting the level
do not count.

The 77 papers that consume the conformal output use prediction sets or conformal
$p$-values as inputs to selection, active learning, planning and similar procedures. Using the sets as data
has its own limit. The law of the prediction sets does not identify a classifier's
confusion matrix once a set of size two or more occurs, and a conformal correlation
matrix built from set membership inherits the failure \citep{cotton2026correlation}.

As an independent check, we coded by hand a random sample of 100 papers, drawn with a
fixed seed from a wider archive of conformal prediction papers, without reference to
any other labels. We find that 93 of the 100 treat the conformal step as terminal. The
remaining 7 are unclear because they pass its output downstream as a filter or a
feature. None fits a further law on the transformed scale and carries it back as a
forecast. Sixty-two of the 100 are in the census, and the two codings agree on 61 of
them.

The argument of this paper does not depend on the exact count. We report it because the uniformity it shows is alarming, highlighting a potential blind spot that
might be unique to the conformal prediction community. Outside conformal prediction the composing move is routine. The normal quantile
transform in hydrological forecasting \citep{bogner2012}, copula time-series
models \citep{chenfan2006}, and autoregressive transformation models
\citep{rugamer2023} all apply an empirical marginal map and keep modelling in
the transformed coordinates.

The readers also recorded how each method pools calibration scores. Of the 623 papers
that propose a method, 452 pool all scores together, 77 pool within fixed strata, and
94 let the weights depend on the test point, through kernel localization,
covariate-shift likelihood ratios or a sampling design.

\section{The conformal information gap}\label{sec:floor}

A \emph{nonconformity score} is a map $A:\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}\to
\mathbb{R}$ assigning a scalar to each candidate outcome $y$ at input $x$. A
\emph{stratification} $\kappa:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{K}$ assigns each input to
a category. Both are fit on a training fold and frozen, since exchangeability
of calibration and test scores is what the coverage argument uses. The schemes
covered by the proposition are those whose score is, for each $x$, a strictly
increasing absolutely continuous bijection in $y$: signed-residual and
normalized signed-residual schemes, and predictive systems built on invertible
score transformations. Folded scores such as the absolute residual
$|y-\widehat\mu(x)|$ and quantile-regression scores are not bijections in $y$.
Remark~\ref{rem:conditional} treats the absolute residual in its symmetric
reading. Quantile-regression scores need a separate treatment. The empirical
arms in Section~\ref{sec:price} are of the covered type.

Such a scheme emits the pooled empirical law of calibration scores in the test
point's stratum, pushed back through $A(x,\cdot)^{-1}$. For log scoring the
emitted law must have a density, so let $\widehat G_\kappa$ be an absolutely
continuous estimate of the pooled score law (a kernel-smoothed or interpolated
empirical law). The raw empirical measure is atomic and would score $-\infty$. Write
$W=A(X,Y)$, let $G_\kappa$ be the population pooled law, and let the benchmark
be the conditional oracle, whose expected negative log score is the conditional
entropy $H(Y\given X)$. Throughout, $L(\widehat F)=\E[-\log \widehat f_X(Y)]$
is the expected log loss of the conformal predictive, so an excess is positive
and smaller is better.

The proof of the floor uses one standard identity, the chain rule for relative
entropy along a product reference \citep{cover2006elements}. In coding theory it
splits redundancy into a dependence term and a mismatch term
\citep{clarke1990,grunwald2007}.

\begin{lemma}[Chain rule for relative entropy]\label{lem:chain}
Let $(U,V)$ have a joint density with $\I(U;V)<\infty$, let $p_V$ be the marginal
density of $V$, and let $q$ be any density with $\E|\log q(V)|<\infty$. Then
\[
\E\log\frac{p(V\given U)}{q(V)} \;=\; \I(U;V)+\KLop\!\big(P_V\,\|\,Q\big).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Insert the marginal density $p_V$ inside the logarithm:
\[
\E\log\frac{p(V\given U)}{q(V)}
\;=\;\E\log\frac{p(V\given U)}{p_V(V)}\;+\;\E\log\frac{p_V(V)}{q(V)} .
\]
The first expectation is $\I(U;V)$ by definition. In the second the integrand
depends on $V$ alone, and $V$ has law $P_V$, so it equals
\[
\int p_V(v)\log\frac{p_V(v)}{q(v)}\,dv \;=\;\KLop\!\big(P_V\,\|\,Q\big).
\]
Both terms are finite under the assumptions.
\end{proof}

The floor needs two further facts about conformal schemes. The first is that the
Jacobian of an invertible score cancels between truth and forecast, so the input
reaches the predictive only through the frozen score and stratum. The second is that
every calibration operation acts on the pooled scores alone. Together they give the
following result, which to our knowledge has not been stated before.

\begin{proposition}[Conformal information gap identity]\label{prop:floor}
Fix the training fold and the calibration sample, so $(A,\kappa,\widehat
G_\kappa)$ are deterministic, let $(X,Y)$ be a test pair independent of both,
and let $W=A(X,Y)$ have conditional density $p_W(\cdot\given x)$ given $X=x$ and
pooled density $g_\kappa$ given the stratum. Assume $\E|\log p_W(W\given X)|$,
$\E|\log g_{\kappa(X)}(W)|$ and $\E|\log\widehat g_{\kappa(X)}(W)|$ are finite, so that
every term below is finite. Then the excess log loss of the conformal predictor
decomposes exactly as
\[
L(\widehat F) - H(Y\given X)
\;=\; \I(W;X\given\kappa)
\;+\; \E_{\kappa}\,\KLop\!\big(G_{\kappa}\,\|\,\widehat G_{\kappa}\big),
\]
where $\I(W;X\given\kappa)$ is the conditional mutual information between score
and input given the stratum. We call it the conformal information gap, or the floor. (Unconditionally, take the outer expectation over
the calibration sample. The first term is unchanged.) The second term is
nonnegative and is the only part any calibration choice, estimator, quantile
convention, randomization, or level, can affect. At a fixed calibration sample
it vanishes if and only if $\widehat G_\kappa=G_\kappa$ almost everywhere.
KL-consistent estimation of the pooled law with growing calibration mass per
stratum drives it to zero asymptotically, which is an assumption on the
estimator, not a consequence of the setup.
\end{proposition}

The proof is a change of variables in which the Jacobian of $A$ cancels between
truth and forecast, then Lemma~\ref{lem:chain} within each stratum
(Appendix~\ref{app:floor}). The input reaches the predictive only through the
frozen score and the frozen stratum. Whatever information about the score the
input still carries within a stratum is therefore lost before calibration begins,
and more calibration data does not recover it. With
a location score and one stratum the floor is $\I(R;X)$, the mutual information
between the model's error and its input.

Figure~\ref{fig:gap} draws this case when the spread of the error grows with the
input. The pooled law is too wide where the noise is low and too narrow where it is
high. With $X$ uniform on $[0,4]$ and error standard deviation $0.15+0.7x$, the floor
is $\I(R;X)=0.243$ nats per observation, and no calibration choice reduces it.

\begin{remark}[When the floor is zero]\label{rem:usecase}
The floor is zero exactly when the frozen pair renders the score independent of
the input within strata: $\I(W;X\given\kappa)=0$ iff $W\perp X\given\kappa$.
When $X$ carries the full information set available downstream, that is the
statement that the modelling was finished before the conformal step. Conformal
calibration is especially valuable when one is unwilling to assume that. Its
guarantee does not diagnose whether the floor is zero, however, and one may deploy
it on perfectly finished residuals purely for finite-sample validity. When the floor is
positive, no calibration choice recovers any of it.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}[Convergence of the pooled law]\label{lem:ident}
Let the calibration pairs be i.i.d.\ copies of $(X,Y)$, or a stationary ergodic
sequence with that marginal law, and let $\widehat G_{\kappa,n}$ be the empirical
distribution of the scores in stratum $\kappa$. Then
$\sup_w|\widehat G_{\kappa,n}(w)-G_\kappa(w)|\to0$ almost surely. If the smoothed
estimate is KL-consistent, the excess log loss tends to the floor
$\I(W;X\given\kappa)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
In the i.i.d.\ case the uniform convergence is the Glivenko--Cantelli theorem. In
the stationary ergodic case the scores $W_i=A(X_i,Y_i)$ are themselves stationary and
ergodic, so the ergodic theorem applied to the indicators $\mathbf 1\{W_i\le w\}$
gives almost sure convergence at each $w$ in a countable dense set. Because
distribution functions are monotone and $G_\kappa$ is continuous, convergence on a
dense set extends to uniform convergence. The second statement follows from
Proposition~\ref{prop:floor}, whose second term tends to zero under KL-consistency.
\end{proof}

Exchangeability alone does not give Lemma~\ref{lem:ident}. An infinite exchangeable
sequence is a mixture of i.i.d.\ sequences, and its empirical law converges to that of
the random directing measure. Coverage therefore needs only exchangeability and holds
at every sample size. The log-score statement is exact for every frozen pooled law
(Proposition~\ref{prop:floor}) and asymptotic only in identifying the law a conformal
scheme ends up with.

Following \citet{delsole2004} and \citet{catt2026}, the \emph{forecastability} of $Y$
given $X$ is
\[
\I(Y;X)\;=\;\E\log p(Y\given X)-\E\log p_Y(Y),
\]
the most any forecast can gain over the unconditional density $p_Y$ in expected log
score. The \emph{exploitability} of a forecast is the gain it actually achieves. It is
at most the forecastability and can be negative.

\begin{corollary}[Gain of a conformal scheme over the unconditional law]\label{cor:forecastability}
Under Proposition~\ref{prop:floor}, let $p_Y$ be the unconditional density of $Y$ and
suppose $\I(Y;X)<\infty$. The conformal predictive gains over the unconditional law
\[
\E\log\widehat f_X(Y)-\E\log p_Y(Y)
\;=\;\I(Y;X)-\I(W;X\given\kappa)-\E_\kappa\KLop\!\big(G_\kappa\,\|\,\widehat G_\kappa\big).
\]
The gain is at most $\I(Y;X)-\I(W;X\given\kappa)$, and the frozen score and
stratification fix that bound. It is negative whenever $\I(W;X\given\kappa)>\I(Y;X)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Add and subtract the oracle's expected log score:
\[
\E\log\widehat f_X(Y)-\E\log p_Y(Y)
\;=\;\big[\E\log p(Y\given X)-\E\log p_Y(Y)\big]
\;-\;\big[\E\log p(Y\given X)-\E\log\widehat f_X(Y)\big].
\]
The first bracket is $\I(Y;X)$ by definition. The second bracket is
$L(\widehat F)-H(Y\given X)$, which Proposition~\ref{prop:floor} evaluates as
$\I(W;X\given\kappa)+\E_\kappa\KLop(G_\kappa\,\|\,\widehat G_\kappa)$. Substituting
gives the display, and dropping the nonnegative KL term gives the bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Calibration does not exploit forecastability]\label{rem:calgain}
Calibration moves $\widehat G_\kappa$ toward $G_\kappa$ and so removes the KL term.
That term depends only on the pooled law of the score, and a forecaster whose inputs
carry no information about the outcome gains it just as much. The part of the gain
that depends on the inputs is $\I(Y;X)-\I(W;X\given\kappa)$, and the frozen score sets
it. Calibration therefore exploits none of the forecastability. For example, suppose $Y$ is independent of $X$ but the location model varies with
$x$. The residual then depends on $X$ through the model alone, and a scheme with exact
coverage scores below the unconditional law by $\I(R;X)$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Distributional, localized and folded-score schemes]\label{rem:conditional}
The input can also return through the score. Distributional conformal prediction
\citep{chernozhukov2021distributional} scores by an estimated conditional rank. Its
floor is therefore $\I(W;X)$ for that rank, which is zero when the estimated
conditional law is the true one. It closes the gap through the estimate and then stops.
Density-based, histogram, localized and quantile constructions
\citep{izbicki2020,izbicki2022,sesia2021,guan2023localized,romano2019conformalized,cauchois2021}
likewise lower the floor by what their conditioning retains, and calibration lowers it
in none of them. The folded absolute residual can be read as a symmetric predictive
with density $h_{\mathrm{sym}}(z)=\tfrac12(p_R(z)+p_R(-z))$. The same computation then
adds $\KLop(P_R\,\|\,H_{\mathrm{sym}})\le\log 2$ for the discarded sign.
\end{remark}

The floor measures how the law of the score varies with the input. Whether an input
is typical or unusual under its own marginal law does not enter, and a rare input with
ordinary noise adds nothing to $\I(W;X\given\kappa)$. Marginal coverage also does not
say which inputs are over- or under-covered. We use \emph{coverage} for the property of
a set and \emph{calibration} for the distributional property of a predictive.

\section{The cost of stopping on 17{,}364 economic series}\label{sec:price}

To measure the cost of stopping we need a forecaster in which the repaired map of
Section~\ref{sec:transform} can sit at more than one position. We build one from
the grammar of Table~\ref{tab:transforms} and run it on 17{,}364 economic series.
Stopping at the pooled law costs about 0.4 nats per observation one step ahead,
composing a model after the map recovers a third of that, and interval coverage
does not register the loss.

\subsection{Moving the pooled law within a forecasting chain}\label{sec:grammar}

Each chain in the grammar removes one kind of structure and passes its residual on.
A terminal leaf models the law of whatever residual remains, and the predictive in
the original coordinate is the leaf's law pushed back through the inverse chain.
The base forecaster, \texttt{laplace}, runs roughly sixty candidate chains in
parallel, combines their predictive densities with likelihood weights updated
every observation, splices generalized Pareto tails onto the extremes, and
scores its own probability integral transforms online. It ranks first in the
public benchmark this study extends, and its own terminal leaf is tuned to
CRPS.

We compare two arms. Both use the same object, the kernel-smoothed pooled
empirical law of recent residuals, and they differ only in where it sits.

\begin{description}
\item[stop.] The base forecaster runs unchanged. Its predictive keeps its
  location, and its shape is replaced by the pooled law of the forecaster's own
  recent errors. That is the quoted forecast, and nothing is fitted to it
  afterwards. This arm is a frozen conformal scheme covered by
  Proposition~\ref{prop:floor}, with floor $\I(R;X)$.
\item[compose-after.] The pooled law moves inside the forecaster. In each
  candidate chain $c$ it replaces the terminal leaf, so it describes that
  chain's own residual stream $R^c_t$ instead of the final error. Write
  $\widehat G^c_t$ for that law and $(T^c_t)^{-1}_{\#}$ for pushing a law back
  through chain $c$'s inverse transforms. The arm's predictive is
  \[
  \widehat F_t \;=\; \sum_c w^c_t \,\big(T^c_t\big)^{-1}_{\#}\widehat G^c_t ,
  \]
  where the weight $w^c_t$ is updated every observation from the density chain
  $c$ assigned to the outcome. The mixture is therefore a model fitted to the
  output of the empirical maps. Because every chain ends in an empirical map,
  the weights choose between chains and never between the empirical map and a
  parametric leaf. This arm has no coverage certificate.
\end{description}

We compare the arms by out-of-sample log likelihood, so each must give the
realized outcome a usable density, including outcomes beyond the range of past
residuals, where an empirical law, even kernel-smoothed, has almost none. The base
forecaster and both arms therefore share one further step, a generalized Pareto
splice that replaces the density beyond a fixed tail region. Because all three
share it, the splice does not drive the differences between them. The pooled law
is built from a window of recent residuals, and a stale window would lose likelihood
for reasons unrelated to stopping. Both arms therefore run with flat and with
recency-weighted windows (exponential weights, half-life 150 observations).

\subsection{Series, tiers and the null control}\label{sec:design}

The corpus is FRED economic change series (differences of rates, production,
employment and similar), non-price, with two tiers. The \emph{breadth} tier
uses 256 observations of history per forecast origin and every qualifying
series: 17{,}364. The \emph{deep} tier uses 1{,}024 observations and series with at
least 1{,}100, so the 400-residual window is a strict subset of the history it
could have used: about 6{,}400 series. Each series has twelve rolling origins,
and we forecast at horizons 1, 6 and 12. We score each forecast by its log density, in nats. A
deficit of $0.15$ nats means the realized outcomes receive $e^{0.15}\approx1.16$
times less probability per observation. We also compute CRPS, the continuous ranked
probability score, which is in the units of the data and which most conformal papers
report. Because origins within a
series overlap, all intervals are bootstrap over series, not rows.

We floor the log density at $-25$ per observation. The floor is not a
proper score where it binds, and it binds more often on the stopped arm (1.78\% of
rows) than on the base (0.62\%, breadth tier, paired). That favours the stopped arm.
Tightening the floor stepwise from $-25$ to $-10$ shrinks the raw stop deficit at
horizon 1 monotonically from 0.51 to 0.33 nats, so every gap reported here understates
its unclipped value.

Part of what a constrained arm loses is the cost of estimating an empirical law
from a finite window. To gauge it we run the identical protocol, at matched context,
on a control corpus of synthetic iid draws (Gaussian, Student-$t_3$, Laplace). These
series have no state to condition on, so what a constrained arm loses on them is
estimation overhead alone: the price of a 41-node empirical law instead of a
parametric scale. We subtract that overhead from every headline number below and call
the result \emph{null-control-adjusted}. The adjustment is a gauge, not an exact
correction. The estimation term $\KLop(G\,\|\,\widehat G)$ depends on $G$, and three
simple iid laws need not match economic residuals. The base forecaster also adapts,
so even on iid data its residuals are not exactly state-independent. Without the
adjustment the breadth-tier deficits are 0.51, 0.61 and 0.59 nats at horizons 1, 6
and 12 (paired, 208{,}368 rows per horizon). The adjustment removes about a fifth of
that.

\subsection{Cost of stopping and the share recovered by composing}\label{sec:decomp}

On the 17{,}364 breadth-tier series, the null-control-adjusted log score deficits
against the unconstrained base are as follows.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
 & $h{=}1$ & $h{=}6$ & $h{=}12$ \\
\midrule
stop, total cost            & 0.397 & 0.475 & 0.462 \\
\quad of which pooled shape & 0.267 & 0.315 & 0.275 \\
\quad of which position     & 0.131 & 0.160 & 0.187 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Raw per-arm deficits carry series-bootstrap 95\% intervals of about $\pm 0.02$
throughout. Composing recovers 33\%, 34\% and 40\% of the cost at the three
horizons. The deep tier gives raw stop
costs of 0.34 to 0.40 nats with 40 to 55 percent recovered by the interior
placement.

The deep-tier fraction is not a pure position effect. Between tiers three things
change at once: the context grows from 256 to 1{,}024 observations, the series
panels differ, and the 400-residual window truncates a 1{,}024-observation history
while holding all of a 256-observation one. A stopped map need not use a
400-observation window, since it may retain every residual it has ever seen. A
same-series, same-context ablation would separate these effects, with stop arms at
windows 250, 400, 750 and all history and composed arms matched window for window
on the ctx-1024 panel. Until it runs, we do not know whether the cost of stopping
grows with how much the pooled window discards.

The arms are close on the median series. The mean cost is carried by a
minority of series where stopping hurts badly.

Adaptivity does not substitute for position. Recency-weighting the stop arm's
window recovers 0.05 to 0.08 nats on the breadth tier, against 0.14 to 0.27
for moving the map's position, raw in both cases. Proposition~\ref{prop:floor} dispatches level adaptation
directly, since it acts on the pooled scores alone.

On CRPS the tiers disagree. On the deep tier the stopped arm degrades CRPS and
the composed arm improves it, while on the breadth tier both positions sit within
about 1\% of the base. We do not use CRPS in any summary.

intervals are the 0.1 and 0.9 quantiles of the kernel-smoothed predictive, which
need not coincide with the raw conformal order-statistic endpoints. The table

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Three published results concern the pooled shape. Exact distribution-free
conditional coverage is impossible in nonatomic settings
\citep{lei2014distribution,barber2021limits}. Weighting calibration points unequally
costs coverage in proportion to the total-variation distance between their residual
laws \citep{barber2023beyond}. A guarantee held at a fixed point rather than on
average inflates the threshold for classical testing-power reasons
\citep{li1989honest,low1997nonparametric,cai2004adaptation}. Together these give the
pooled shape a defence. None of them concerns the stop.

Training a neural network stops at an epoch chosen on validation evidence. The
conformal recipe stops at its empirical map with no such evidence, and the census
shows the stop is near universal, so we call it \emph{cultural}. One likely reason is
that the certificate belongs to the stop. Coverage holds at the empirical map, so
work that continues past it gives up the property the field measures itself by, and
the field's effort has gone instead into better weights, strata and levels.

A deficit of 0.4 nats means that the stopped forecast gives the realized outcome, on
geometric average, about two-thirds of the density the unconstrained forecast gives
it, and the coverage that conformal practice monitors registers none of this.

We call the stop a permanent structural information sacrifice, and both adjectives
are meant literally. The sacrifice is structural because the score and the strata fix
it before any calibration sample is drawn. It is permanent because nothing applied to
the pooled scores, neither a level schedule nor a larger calibration sample, recovers
it. The remedies lie elsewhere: upstream, in a score that leaves less of the input in
the residual, or downstream, in a model fitted to the map's output, which is what the
composed arm does.

Composing does give up the certificate, but the identity also prices getting it back.
A final empirical map placed at the end of the composed chain restores the
certificate, and Proposition~\ref{prop:floor}, applied to that map with the composed
forecaster held fixed, puts its floor at the information left
in the composed chain's residual, not in the base forecaster's. A certificate can
therefore sit wherever it is wanted, as one transform among those of
Table~\ref{tab:transforms}, with the modelling done on either side of it. The model
can be anything, a Bayesian posterior predictive included.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The census labels come from language-model readers, and the
boundaries between stopping, replacing and consuming involve judgment. The two full
readings disagree on 24 of the 680 papers. The prices are measured on one base
forecaster and one economic corpus. The floor proposition covers invertible signed
scores, and the absolute residual only in the symmetric reading of
Remark~\ref{rem:conditional}. Quantile scores need their own treatment.

\section{The repaired map}\label{app:repair}

The piecewise-linear repair interpolates the pairs $(r_{(i)},z_{(i)})$ and extends
beyond the edge knots linearly in $(r,z)$. Linearity in the probit coordinate is a
Gaussian tail in the score, with scale set by the spacing of the extreme knots. The
kernel-smoothed repair replaces the step function \eqref{eq:fencepost} by a
Gaussian-kernel estimate of the distribution function and represents it by a
monotone $C^1$ cubic through the same knots. Both are strictly increasing
bijections of the real line \citep{cotton2026grammar}.

The certificate survives any strictly increasing $m_n$ through the knots. Let
$R_1,\dots,R_{n+1}$ be exchangeable and almost surely distinct. Monotonicity gives
\[
\{m_n(R_{n+1})\le z_{(i)}\}\;=\;\{R_{n+1}\le r_{(i)}\}.
\]
By exchangeability the rank of $R_{n+1}$ among all $n+1$ scores is uniform on
$\{1,\dots,n+1\}$, and $R_{n+1}\le r_{(i)}$ holds for exactly the $i$ lowest ranks,
so
\[
\Pr\{m_n(R_{n+1})\le z_{(i)}\}\;=\;\frac{i}{n+1},\qquad i=1,\dots,n .
\]
Write $U=\Phi(m_n(R_{n+1}))$. For $u$ between consecutive grid levels $i/(n+1)$ and
$(i+1)/(n+1)$, the display and monotonicity of the distribution function give
\[
\frac{i}{n+1}\;\le\;\Pr\{U\le u\}\;\le\;\frac{i+1}{n+1},
\]
so $\Pr\{U\le u\}$ and $u$ lie in the same cell of width $1/(n+1)$. The edge cells
are the same argument with endpoints $0$ and $1$. Hence
$\sup_u|\Pr\{U\le u\}-u|\le 1/(n+1)$, whatever the interpolation, the tails and the
law of the scores.

\section{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:floor}}\label{app:floor}

Fix the fold, so $(A,\kappa)$ is deterministic. Given $X=x$, change of
variables gives the true density $p(y\given x) = p_W(A(x,y)\given x)\,A'(x,y)$,
and the conformal density is $\widehat f_x(y) = \widehat
g_{\kappa(x)}(A(x,y))\,A'(x,y)$ with $\widehat g_\kappa$ the density of the
absolutely continuous estimate $\widehat G_\kappa$. The Jacobian cancels in the
log ratio, so
\[
\KLop\big(P(\cdot\given x)\,\|\,\widehat F_x\big)
= \KLop\big(P_W(\cdot\given x)\,\|\,\widehat G_{\kappa(x)}\big)
= \KLop\big(P_W(\cdot\given x)\,\|\,G_{\kappa(x)}\big)
+ \E_{W\sim P_W(\cdot\given x)}\log\frac{g_{\kappa(x)}(W)}{\widehat g_{\kappa(x)}(W)} .
\]
Averaging over $X$, the first term is
$\E_X\,\KLop(P_W(\cdot\given X)\,\|\,P_W(\cdot\given\kappa(X))) =
\I(W;X\given\kappa)$ by definition. Given $\kappa(X)=k$ the score has law $G_k$,
so the second term averages to
\[
\E_\kappa\,\E_{W\sim G_\kappa}\log\frac{g_\kappa(W)}{\widehat g_\kappa(W)}
\;=\;\E_\kappa \KLop\!\big(G_\kappa\,\|\,\widehat G_\kappa\big)\;\ge\;0 .
\]
Every calibration
operation is a function of the pooled scores alone, hence acts only on
$\widehat G_\kappa$ and the second term. \hfill$\square$
