On authority

Who is an expert in conformal prediction?

To be an expert in the application of conformal prediction, you need to know when to perform the trivial certification. That means knowing when the modeling has finished: when the residuals in front of you are an adequate approximation of whatever assumptions you are making at the moment you claim the certificate, exchangeability above all. And that determination is not a conformal skill. It is expertise in conditional prediction, otherwise known as prediction, together with whatever corners of applied mathematics, machine learning, and statistics bear on the series at hand — ideally sharpened by decades of work where money was on the line. The certification step itself is counting cells.

I personally would be highly suspicious of anyone calling themselves an expert in conformal prediction, because it sounds a lot like being an expert in Scholar’s Mate.

These days, everyone should use the best available LLM to carefully assist in judging the veracity of arguments on purely logical grounds. Terence Tao does. The machines are patient, they are increasingly good at mathematics, and they do not care who is speaking. That last property matters, because argument from authority holds a long-standing place on the list of famous logical fallacies, and nothing about the economics of academic credentialing has repealed it. Every claim on these pages is stated so that it can be checked without reference to its author: the demonstrations run in your browser, and the papers carry their proofs.

You may instead choose to ignore everything offered by busy people working privately in prediction, and read only peer-reviewed work. That is a valid choice. Some of the work here is under submission, and you can wait. But to wait, in an age when a machine will verify the mathematics for you in an afternoon, is to open a different kind of information gap — one that, unlike the other kind, is entirely self-imposed.
