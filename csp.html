Review

Conformal Seasonal Pools: one good tool

Conformal Seasonal Pools (CSP) forecasts a series by drawing from its own past at the same phase of the cycle, around a seasonal-naive forecast. It is training-free, fast and well calibrated where a series repeats itself. This page explains where it wins and where it cannot, and what that says about conformal prediction as a basis for time-series modelling.

The method

Valery Manokhin's CSP (arXiv:2605.03789, csp-forecaster) forms its predictive from two pools: past values from the same phase of the cycle (the same hour of the day, the same week of the year), and signed residuals around a seasonal-naive forecast. It mixes the two, weights recent history more, and samples. Nothing is trained.

Common sense: why it wins where it wins

Take hourly electricity demand. At 6 pm tomorrow, demand will look a lot like it did at 6 pm today and at 6 pm last week. The series returns to roughly the same level at the same time every day. For a series like that, a good summary of what 6 pm will bring is the spread of what 6 pm has brought before. That is exactly what CSP samples.

No model is needed to learn the cycle, because the pool already contains it, and calibration comes almost for free because the pool is made of the very outcomes being forecast. On the strongly seasonal M4 hourly series, CSP beat laplace by CRPS on most series in our July comparison, and laplace adopted a seasonal anchor as a result. Where the past at the same phase is a good sample of the future, CSP is hard to beat.

Common sense: why one tool is not enough

Most series do not return to the same level every day. An unemployment rate drifts for years, then jumps. A spread is calm for months, then volatile for weeks. An index trends. For series like these, the empirical distribution of past values, or of past residuals around a seasonal naive, is a description of a regime that has moved on. The pool is stale exactly when the forecast matters.

A general forecaster needs more than one tool: something to track a moving level, something to follow a trend, something for a scale that changes, something for mean reversion, something for heavy tails. laplace carries about sixty such chains of transforms and lets the data weight them; the skaters methodology guide describes each one. The playground lets you generate a series (trending, seasonal, shifting) and watch, live, which transform chains the ensemble leans on as its character changes. On FRED economic series, laplace beat CSP on 77 to 99 percent of series by CRPS and 90 to 100 percent by log score in the same July comparison, across every corpus and every CSP configuration tried.

The empirical distribution is one of those tools, not a rival to them. With only that tool, the forecaster wins where the series repeats and loses where it does not.

Conformal prediction is (sometimes) early stopping

CSP's pools are conformal in spirit: the forecast is read off an empirical distribution of past outcomes or past scores. Marginally Useful: Conformal Prediction Is Early Stopping at an Information Price studies what that step costs.

Viewed as a map, the conformal step is a univariate transform in need of repair. In raw form it is a step function with no inverse and no density. Once repaired by interpolation it is an invertible transform, and it can sit anywhere in a chain alongside subtracting a running mean, dividing by a running scale or differencing.

Whether conformal prediction is early stopping depends on what comes after it. If the conformal map is the last step, so that the forecast is the pooled empirical law of the scores, modelling has stopped there. If a model is fitted to the map's output, it has not. Stopping is the norm: of 623 papers proposing conformal methods surveyed there, only two go past the map.

On 17,364 economic series, stopping at the pooled law costs about 0.4 nats per observation one step ahead, and interval coverage does not register the loss. Composing a model after the map recovers about a third of it.

Depending on the construction, the stop is an information gambit: the score and stratification, chosen before any calibration data is seen, decide what information about the input survives into the forecast. The paper's conformal information gap identity prices what they discard, and no amount of calibration wins it back. The paper calls this a permanent structural information sacrifice.

So: a transform, not a basis

The empirical distribution, conformal calibration included, is a useful transform like all the others. CSP shows how useful it is on series that repeat. It is not, by itself, a basis for general time-series modelling: that needs the other tools too, and it needs modelling to continue past the conformal step when there is something left to model.

Results here are from the July 2026 comparison (laplace vs CSP), which used the author's package installed from GitHub as its README directs. A rerun on about 18,800 series under the same protocol as the foundation models will appear on foundationalprediction.com.
