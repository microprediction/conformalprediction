Review

Conformal correlation

A 2026 paper in Computer Communications correlates class membership across conformal prediction sets and offers the result as a rigorous and privacy preserving successor to the confusion matrix. We read the paper closely and rebuilt the statistic on a classifier with known confusability. The construction is a threshold on the softmax followed by second moments. The coverage certificate transfers nothing to it, and its entries measure class prevalence more than confusability. Each point below is exhibited live in demonstration 32.

What is proposed

Perlo, Chiasserini, De Veciana and Malandrino train an image classifier, hold out a labeled calibration set, and build adaptive prediction sets (APS) at miscoverage level \(\alpha\). For each test image they record an indicator vector \(z = (z_1, \dots, z_H)\), with \(z_h = 1\) when class \(h\) is in the set. The conformal correlation matrix (CCM) has entries

$$\rho_{ij} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[z_i z_j] - \mathbb{E}[z_i]\,\mathbb{E}[z_j]} {\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(z_i)\,\operatorname{Var}(z_j)}}.$$

The authors read positive entries as classes the model confuses, negative entries as classes it keeps apart, and entries near zero as unrelated. They claim that the matrix sees potential errors where the confusion matrix sees only realized ones, that it can be estimated from unlabeled data, that it discloses less than a confusion matrix in federated training, and that it improves client selection when the goal is to separate two critical classes. The abstract describes it as capturing “the likelihood of class pairs co-occurring within conformal prediction sets.”

What is computed

An APS set is the softmax vector sorted, cumulated, and cut at one scalar threshold. The threshold is the \((1-\alpha)\) quantile of the calibration scores, but any number in \([0,1]\) would produce a set of the same kind. Therefore everything in the matrix is a function of the classifier’s probability vectors and one cutoff. The probabilities already hold, at full resolution, what the indicator vector reports at one bit per class.

The authors write that the entries “incorporate the entire distribution of predicted probabilities.” They incorporate one threshold of it. The authors then sweep \(\alpha\) over four values and list the sweep as a benefit, because the operator “can specify how near the near misses have to be.” The sweep recovers, in four coarse steps, what the threshold discarded.

The natural comparators are older than conformal prediction. The soft confusion matrix is the mean probability vector assigned to images of each true class. It sees near misses at every resolution and needs no calibration set. The mean pairwise product \(\mathbb{E}[p_i p_j]\) is the threshold free version of \(\mathbb{E}[z_i z_j]\). The authors cite the coverage confusion matrix of Stutz et al. (ICLR 2022) in their related work. None of the three appears in an experiment. The only comparison offered is with the hard confusion matrix, which discards the probabilities entirely, so the contrast being drawn is soft versus hard rather than conformal versus not.

The certificate does not travel

The one theorem in play says the true class lands in the set at rate at least \(1-\alpha\), averaged over exchangeable draws. It constrains a single row of the co-membership structure, the row for the true label. It says nothing about which wrong classes accompany the true one, and the matrix is entirely about the wrong classes. Therefore the word “conformal” in the name certifies nothing about the numbers in the matrix, and the authors do not say what it would certify. The calibration step chose a threshold, and that is the whole contribution of conformal prediction to the object.

The guarantee is also misstated. The authors write that “for a given input sample” the set “is guaranteed to contain the correct class” with probability close to \(1-\alpha\). Split conformal coverage is marginal. It is not conditional on the input, and distribution free conditional coverage is impossible in finite samples (Barber, Candès, Ramdas and Tibshirani 2021). The distinction matters here because the authors go on to interpret individual class pairs and individual federated clients as if validity transferred to them. The upper bound is written as \(1/(n-1)\), where the standard term is \(1/(n+1)\). Exchangeability, the tie convention and the randomization in APS are not stated.

In the federated experiments the clients are constructed to be non identically distributed, and local matrices are compared against a global calibration set. That is the mismatch under which the marginal guarantee fails. No empirical coverage and no set sizes are reported for any client.

The entries measure prevalence

Correlation between two Bernoulli indicators, the phi coefficient, is pinned by the marginal rates. Write \(p_i = \mathbb{E}[z_i]\) for the fraction of test sets containing class \(i\). Two classes that never share a set have \(\mathbb{E}[z_i z_j] = 0\), hence

$$\rho_{ij} = -\sqrt{\frac{p_i\, p_j}{(1-p_i)(1-p_j)}}.$$

On a ten class problem with a decent classifier each class sits in roughly a tenth of the sets, and every “hard to confuse” pair reads the same number. With balanced inclusion rates and singleton sets the number is exactly \(-1/(H-1)\).

Remark 2 of the paper says dissimilar classes are “expected to display strong negative correlation.” They display a negative number whose size is set by how often each class is included. That rises with the class’s prevalence and with \(1-\alpha\), and it has nothing to do with how dissimilar the classes are. The same pinning acts at the top. The phi coefficient cannot reach \(+1\) unless the two marginals are equal, so a rare class and a common class have a lower attainable ceiling than two common classes. Entries for pairs with different inclusion rates are therefore not on a common scale. Panel A of the demonstration draws the observed floor and the formula on top of each other.

Remark 1, that positive entries mark “intrinsically similar classes,” fails from the other side. Any input the classifier finds hard produces a large set, and every label in a large set is positively correlated with every other. Raise the share of hard inputs in panel A and all 45 pairs turn positive, including the 44 the classifier separates perfectly. That is a set size effect, and the authors offer no adjustment for it. The statistic is also undefined whenever a class is always or never included, since its variance is zero, and no convention is given.

The abstract promised a likelihood of co-occurrence. The co-occurrence rate \(\mathbb{E}[z_i z_j]\) is a likelihood. Dividing it by the marginals is what introduced the prevalence dependence. The \(\alpha\) dependence the authors present as a feature is in part this mechanical effect: at higher \(\alpha\) the sets shrink, every \(p_i\) falls, and every negative entry drifts toward zero whether or not the classifier changed. Panel C shows the other cost. As the sets shrink toward singletons the matrix loses the ability to rank pairs by their true confusability, while the probability based statistic keeps it at every \(\alpha\).

Symmetric object, directional claim

The confusion matrix is asymmetric. It can say that images of class 91 are labeled 30 while images of class 30 are never labeled 91. The CCM is a correlation matrix and is symmetric by construction. The authors nevertheless read from it that the classifier “is much more likely to misclassify images of class 91 as class 30 than as class 95,” and later that the matrix “contains a superset of the information conveyed by the confusion matrix.” Neither can be true. A symmetric matrix has \(H(H-1)/2\) distinct off diagonal entries, and directional confusion between class pairs needs \(H(H-1)\). The evaluation never sees a label, so nothing in it can distinguish a set \(\{1, 2\}\) produced for a true 1, which is a correct prediction, from the same set produced for a true 2, which is an error. The two cases give identical matrices and can both have perfect coverage. Panel B slides the direction of a confusion from entirely 0 to 1 to entirely 1 to 0. The soft confusion entries cross, and the CCM entry is a curve symmetric about the midpoint.

Labels and privacy

The authors claim the matrix “can be estimated entirely from unlabeled samples.” The test set is unlabeled. The calibration set that sets the threshold is not, and in the federated experiments it is a tenth of every client’s local data. A softmax statistic needs labels at no step.

The privacy argument compares a correlation matrix, which “only contains probabilities,” with a raw count confusion matrix, whose row sums reveal the size and class composition of a client’s data. A row normalized confusion matrix also only contains probabilities. The CCM is not free of composition either. By the formula above, the off diagonal entries of a well trained classifier are near \(-\sqrt{p_i p_j/((1-p_i)(1-p_j))}\), which is a function of the inclusion rates, which track the class frequencies. The client that sends its CCM has sent a blurred copy of its label histogram. There is no threat model, no privacy mechanism, no leakage bound and no inference attack evaluated. The matrix is recommended precisely because it exposes how a client’s data differs from the others. The defensible claim is that the CCM does not transmit raw counts.

The experiment

The client selection section contradicts the rest of the paper. In the centralized experiments a high entry means two classes are hard to distinguish, and the entry falling with training is read as the classifier learning to separate them. In the federated section the stated goal is “to make the corresponding CCMs to be as high as possible,” and the rule selects “the 7 clients with the highest CCM entries for the critical classes.” The rationale that follows is that a client “unable to distinguish the critical classes” is unlikely to help. By the authors’ own reading, the highest entries belong to exactly those clients. The sign flips without explanation.

The rest of the section has similar problems. The heterogeneity is generated with a Dirichlet whose concentration is described as producing more heterogeneous clients as it grows. For the symmetric Dirichlet it is the reverse. Figures 6 and 7 are captioned “CCM entries for the critical classes,” and their vertical axes are labeled class accuracy. The text selects three clients per round from a pool of seven, and the table reports the number selected as six or seven. “Significant” is used throughout with one run, no seeds and no error bars.

There is no baseline that could attribute anything to the conformal step. Random selection with the same pool size, selection by confusion matrix, by class counts, by local loss, by co-membership rate or by soft probability are all absent. A client whose data contains more of the two focus classes will have a larger local entry for that pair, so the rule may be selecting on class composition and nothing else.

The conclusion says the method improves the critical classes “without hurting the overall classification performance” and “does not affect the performance for non-critical classes.” Table 1 says otherwise.

That is a redistribution of accuracy toward the weaker focus class, paid for by the stronger one and, on CIFAR-100, by every other class. It might be a defensible trade for an application that cares about one class above all others. The authors do not present it as a trade, and with one run they cannot say whether any of the differences would survive a second seed.

No uncertainty is attached to any entry of any matrix. The coverage guarantee says nothing about sampling error in a correlation estimated from a few hundred local test images, and neither do the authors. APS sets are also randomized to hit exact coverage, so the indicators carry noise from the randomization that a softmax statistic does not.

What a fair version would look like

If the goal is a picture of which classes the model cannot tell apart, use the probabilities. The soft confusion matrix is directional, threshold free and label free. If pairwise structure is wanted, \(\mathbb{E}[p_i p_j]\) or the correlation of log probabilities across test images does not suffer the Bernoulli pinning. If sets are genuinely the object, because a downstream consumer receives sets and nothing else, then report the co-membership rate \(\mathbb{E}[z_i z_j]\) or the coverage confusion matrix of Stutz et al., which is directional because it conditions on the true label. In either case say plainly that the certificate covers the true class and nothing about the neighbors, and report the inclusion rates and set sizes beside whatever is reported, since a phi coefficient cannot be interpreted without them.

The habit

The authors did not stop at the conformal step. They went past it, into a second order statistic, which is the move this site argues for. However, they took the set as the object and threw away the probabilities to get it. The packaging teaches that the set is the certified thing, and so anything built on the set is assumed to inherit the certificate. Nothing was inherited. What traveled was a threshold, chosen by a procedure whose one theorem concerns a quantity the matrix ignores.

The conformal step is a univariate transform. Used as a transform, its output is a coordinate that can be modeled further. Used as a coarsening, it is a lossy binarization with a certificate attached to the one bit that the downstream analysis then discards.
